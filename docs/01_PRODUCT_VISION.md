# Product Vision

## Positioning

F1 Intelligence is not intended to be another high-volume Formula 1 news site. Its core job is to help readers understand how an F1 story develops across sessions, races and the season.

Working positioning:

> Follow the story, not just the news.

A conventional news page answers: **What happened?**

F1 Intelligence should increasingly answer:

- What changed?
- Why does it matter?
- What evidence supports that interpretation?
- How does this connect to previous races or technical changes?
- What should we watch next?

## Target users

Primary audience:

- English-speaking F1 fans who already follow race weekends but want more context than headlines provide.
- Fans interested in car development, upgrades, regulations, strategy and performance trends.
- Readers who want a concise post-session or post-race explanation without monitoring dozens of sources themselves.

Secondary audience:

- More technical fans who want a chronological record of team development.
- Returning readers who follow one team, driver or storyline across the season.
- Newsletter readers who want a high-signal daily or race-weekend catch-up.

## Product pillars

### 1. Story continuity

A story is not a single article. A canonical Story Object persists while new evidence, quotes, technical changes and race outcomes are attached to it.

Example:

`Red Bull rear stability problem`

may contain evidence from several Grands Prix, driver comments, setup changes, upgrade packages and technical directives. Individual articles are views onto that longer-running story.

### 2. Evidence-linked analysis

Analysis should distinguish clearly between:

- documented fact,
- source claim,
- observed data,
- community discussion,
- editorial interpretation.

The system should preserve source provenance so that conclusions can be revisited when new evidence appears.

### 3. Technical development tracking

Team development is one of the strongest long-lived content opportunities. The platform should make it easy to answer questions such as:

- What has McLaren changed since Australia?
- When did Ferrari introduce its current floor concept?
- Which upgrades preceded a performance improvement?
- Which parts were circuit-specific rather than permanent?

### 4. Race-weekend intelligence

Each Grand Prix should behave like a living hub rather than a set of disconnected articles.

Typical lifecycle:

- pre-event context,
- practice analysis,
- qualifying analysis,
- race analysis,
- post-race synthesis,
- links back into longer-running team and technical stories.

### 5. Evergreen explanation

News creates spikes; explainers and trackers create durable search traffic.

Examples:

- undercut vs overcut,
- how F1 floors generate downforce,
- parc ferme rules,
- tyre degradation,
- brake migration,
- 2026 regulations,
- team upgrade timelines.

## Differentiation

The intended advantage is not raw publishing speed. Large publishers will usually win that race.

The advantage should come from combining multiple source types into structured context:

- FIA documents,
- official team material,
- driver and team-principal comments,
- session and race data where legally usable,
- established journalism,
- community discussion as a signal source rather than a factual authority,
- internal historical knowledge from earlier race weekends.

The platform should become more useful as its archive grows.

## What we should not build

We should avoid becoming:

- a generic AI news rewrite site,
- a feed of isolated press-release summaries,
- a volume-first SEO content farm,
- a clone of existing live-timing products,
- a product whose value depends on copyrighted F1 imagery or branding we do not control.

## Editorial principle

Automation should reduce research and synthesis cost, not remove judgment.

Preferred workflow:

`Sources -> Evidence -> Story cluster -> Analysis brief -> Editorial output`

Avoid:

`Source -> LLM rewrite -> Auto-publish`

## Initial success definition

The first meaningful product milestone is not revenue. It is demonstrating that the site can repeatedly create pages readers return to because they preserve and explain context better than a standard news feed.

Early product signals:

- users navigate from a current article into older story context,
- race hubs receive repeat visits across a weekend,
- team development pages gain organic search impressions,
- newsletter signups come from analysis pages rather than only promotional pages,
- returning-user share increases over time.
